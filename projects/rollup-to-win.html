Tim Hortons Roll Up to Win

Rebuilding the digital game to eliminate technical friction and simplify the user journey.

Overview

Roll Up to Win is Tim Hortons' flagship contest, played by millions of Guests each season. I led the rebuild of the digital game — eliminating technical friction and simplifying the user journey — lifting engagement 15.3% year over year and onboarding 3.1M users in the 2026 season.

RoleLead Designer & Illustrator/Animator
Team2 Product Managers, 5 Developers, Head of Product and Head of Engineering
Timeline2025–2026

The Challenge

The Roll Up to Win digital game had stagnated since 2020, with no mechanical or interface updates. By 2025, the hybrid physical/digital gameplay model caused engagement to drop to a five-year low.

Research & Problem Statement

We approached the problem from three research angles:

  • Usability testing with returning players — we recruited Guests who had played Roll Up to Win in previous years, so we could watch where the existing experience lost people rather than guess
  • Survey results from Tims Insiders — our Guest research panel gave us attitudinal data at scale on what players found frustrating and what kept them coming back
  • App store review analysis — we compiled and categorized App Store and Google Play reviews specifically mentioning Roll Up to Win, surfacing unprompted complaints from real players mid-season

All three sources converged on the same pain points:

  • Sluggish performance and technical glitches
  • Navigation complexity with excessive pages
  • Extended load times
  • Insufficient engagement
  • Cluttered information architecture
  • Friction in continuing gameplay
How might we rebuild the digital experience to eliminate technical friction and simplify the user journey?

Approach

We discovered the engine was loading four heavy pages simultaneously. The strategy emphasized a "simplicity-first" philosophy — reducing time-to-reward and prioritizing the psychological payoff moment.

Mapping the journey made the real problem obvious. To play, a user had to:

Before

Navigate → load → read → confirm → play → navigate → play again

Goal

Open → play → reward → play again

That's the core product insight. We weren't merely simplifying the UI — we were shortening the reward loop. Every decision that followed was measured against a single question: does this move the Guest closer to the payoff moment, or further from it?

Design Solutions

1. Onboarding

Users were forced through five informational screens before reaching the game's primary action, delaying the first moment of play. 5 screens down to 1 screen, bringing the time to first roll lower than previous years.

2. Skill Testing Question (STQ)

Shifted from an immediate requirement post-signup to "just-in-time" deployment — appearing only when users attempt their first roll. The principle: don't ask users to solve a problem before they have encountered it.

Instead of

Sign up → STQ → finally play

We created

Sign up → play → STQ when required

3. Reducing Navigation & Load Complexity

Eliminated the four-tab structure; consolidated "Play Now" and "Timeline" into a paginated feed; moved "Prizes" to modal access.

4. Prize Clarity

Guests were confused about the odds — with app-based and cup-based prizes pooled into one list, it wasn't clear what they were actually playing for. Introducing a toggle view that separated the two into distinct tabs resolved the confusion, and it visually demonstrated that there were far more digital prizes to be won in the app than on the physical cup.

5. Engagement Loop

Reduced multi-roll gameplay from five clicks per entry to one "Roll Again" button on the results screen.

Reflection

The biggest lesson was that improving engagement wasn't about adding more game mechanics. It was about removing everything between the user and the reward.

Every instinct on a stagnating product points toward addition — new modes, new mechanics, more reasons to come back. The research pointed the opposite direction. Players weren't asking for more game; they were telling us the game was buried under screens, loads, and confirmations. The wins came from subtraction: five onboarding screens became one, a four-tab structure collapsed into a single feed, and a five-click re-roll became one button.

It also reframed how I think about performance. Load time wasn't an engineering footnote to clean up at the end — it was a design problem, because every second between opening the app and rolling is a second the Guest can decide it isn't worth it. Treating speed as part of the experience, and partnering with engineering early to find those constraints, mattered as much as any screen I designed.
